Readable Studio

An HTML document tool. AI writes the draft, and people edit it directly, like in PowerPoint.

Why should one typo or a 10-pixel nudge mean another round of chatting with the AI?

  1. Find

    A report-format dilemma. Word is easy for AI to handle but weak on visual emphasis; PowerPoint is the opposite. The more AI reads and continues documents, the wider that gap gets.

  2. Dig

    HTML can do both. Thariq’s post “The unreasonable effectiveness of HTML”, from the Claude Code team, gave the clue: with HTML, people and AI read the same file, and it’s expressive enough for reports.

  3. Build

    The bottleneck was the last touch-ups, not generation. Even one typo meant another prompt. I forked the open-source open-design (Apache-2.0) and added an editing layer that writes on-screen edits straight back to the HTML source.

  4. Land

    Released, and refined through my own use. It ships as a portable Windows build and is under review for internal use.

DetailsHide
  • Edit directly on screen: move, resize, alignment guides and snapping, formatting, color, spacing, borders, opacity, duplicate, delete, undo and redo.
  • Editable elements get stable identifiers, so on-screen changes are patched into the matching node in the HTML source. The AI continues from the source as the person edited it.
  • Each LLM chat leaves a Git commit point. If the AI breaks the output, you roll back to the earlier state.
  • Manual edits are saved or discarded explicitly. The tool keeps a SHA-256 of the source from when editing started. If the file changed in the meantime, it stops the save and leaves the user’s edits intact.
  • I split a huge single JavaScript bundle with code splitting and lazy loading, and removed unused features, so it runs even on slow virtual desktops (VDI).

Automated weekly production plan updates

Slow ERP pulls and manual Excel work, turned into a data pipeline. Figures that arrive by email are filled in automatically too.

  1. Find

    About 2 hours of manual work, every time. Planners downloaded columns one by one from a slow ERP that wouldn’t give data in the shape they needed, then stitched them together by hand in Excel.

  2. Dig

    The data already existed. The problem was how it was fetched and processed. Values the pipeline couldn’t reach were arriving as numbers in the planners’ email.

  3. Build

    A pipeline, pandas, and a rule-based parser. I pulled the data with Databricks and moved the Excel processing into pandas. Email figures go through a rule-based parser, not an LLM: the output sets production volume, so the same input must give the same answer.

  4. Land

    About 2 hours → about 3 minutes. Since deployment, everyone on the planning team updates the plan with this tool.

DetailsHide
  • The question wasn’t “Can we use AI here?” It was “Is nondeterminism acceptable here?”

Raw material purchasing support

A call that should be made daily but was put off to once a week, now a live stock view with order-by dates.

  1. Find

    A daily call, pushed to once a week. Buyers weigh the production plan against raw material stock. Pulling data from slow systems and merging it in Excel pivots took too long to do every day.

  2. Dig

    Do the math before anyone has to. The inputs were recent production, production in the same month last year, current stock, and lead times that vary by origin.

  3. Build

    A live stock view and an order-by date. Each raw material’s stock is charted live, with a dotted line projecting it at the current usage rate. Average lead times come from past orders and receipts; working backward from the risk line gives the date to order by.

  4. Land

    Review the draft, press send. It even drafts the purchase request email; the buyer checks it and sends it. It’s deployed and used for real purchasing decisions.

DetailsHide
  • Automation stops at the inputs and the draft. The buyer does the final send (human-in-the-loop).
  • Solved with data engineering and rule-based math, not generative AI.

SK enmove · AX Team · 2026

Status: In operation · Used in inventory planning

4 months aheadSales forecast per product

Sales demand forecasting

A forecasting problem with no baseline at all. I picked the model that held steady across periods over the one I built.

  1. Find

    No forecast, and nothing to compare against. To prepare production early, the team needed product-level sales several months ahead.

  2. Dig

    Baseline first. I computed exactly what the business already relied on, the 3-month average around the same month last year, and made it the bar. Data errors, like one product under two codes, I traced and fixed one by one with the business team.

  3. Build

    Not my model, the steady one. My own LightGBM won in one evaluation period, but its error swung noticeably when the period changed. Chronos-2, a time-series foundation model, held a similar level in both.

  4. Land

    Used in inventory planning. Backtested over about 90 months split into periods; the forecasts now feed actual inventory planning.

DetailsHide
  • LightGBM’s feature importance was mostly past sales. Being “interpretable” didn’t give the business much new information.
  • Many products sell close to zero, so I evaluated with WAPE on total volume instead of MAPE.
  • While tuning the LightGBM candidate I tried Tweedie loss, RevIN, multiple seeds, and ensembles. That’s separate from the final model’s results.
  • Time series
  • Chronos-2
  • LightGBM
  • Backtesting

Chung-Ang University Cognitive Intelligence Lab · Undergraduate Researcher

2025.10 — 2026.03

Why does CLIP confuse “B on A” with “A on B”?

CLIP often ties on sentences with the same words but flipped relations. I reframed this as a problem with how the score is read, not a lack of representation.

DetailsHide
  • From the covariance of calibration image–text pairs, I built coupling channels with whitening and SVD, and designed a training-free score Ω that uses them.
  • Using Ω as a full replacement for cosine similarity breaks general performance. So it only re-ranks between the top 2 cosine candidates, and only when Ω’s channel evidence is more consistent.
  • About 3 points better on the ARO benchmark with no extra training, while keeping the drop in general retrieval on MSCOCO and Flickr30k under 0.3 points. I wrote it up as a paper draft, covering where it works and where it doesn’t.
  • CLIP
  • VLM
  • Compositionality

Chung-Ang University Multimodal AI Lab · Undergraduate Researcher

2025.02 — 2025.10

Diagnosing a diverged LLaVA run from its logs

A LLaVA-1.5 7B LoRA fine-tune collapsed midway. Before changing any settings, I reread the W&B logs.

DetailsHide
  • Divergence began not right after warmup but while the learning rate sat at its peak, and the loss never recovered. I listed candidates like data, numerical instability, and resume state, and picked the most direct hypothesis: the learning rate was too high.
  • Keeping the schedule, I lowered only the peak LoRA learning rate from 2e-4 to 2e-5. It converged and matched the performance reported in the paper.
  • I fit the public multi-GPU setup onto a single personal GPU (DeepSpeed ZeRO-2, gradient checkpointing), and added vLLM serving, which the official repo lacks, to run the benchmarks.
  • LLaVA
  • LoRA
  • Training stability

Chung-Ang University Super-Intelligence Computing Lab · Researcher · Government project

2024.09 — 2025.02

From “where to build” to “how much it’ll be used”

A public facility siting project for several local governments. Data was scarce and full of gaps, so I reframed location recommendation as demand forecasting per candidate site.

DetailsHide
  • Some areas had unusually high usage for their population. Before deleting them as outliers, I looked closer: they were office districts. I asked the local government for bus boarding data to capture floating population, which improved MAPE by 20 points.
  • Recommendations kept clustering in one spot. I fixed that with iterative forecasting: assume a facility is built at the first pick, then recompute demand.
  • I agreed on input and output specs with the backend developer, connected it to an API that shows recommended sites on a map, and delivered it with a report.

How I work

Principles that keep showing up in the work above.

  1. 01

    If you need to know the tech to use it, it won’t get used

    A tool that moves Excel into a database confirms table relationships in the business team’s words, not PK/FK. The auto-fix pipeline’s final check is a deploy link, not code.

  2. 02

    Decide where AI goes, and where it doesn’t

    Email figures that feed the production plan are parsed with rules, on purpose. Purchase request emails stop at a draft. The buyer sends them.

  3. 03

    Find the best option within the rules, even when locked down

    Where outside access was restricted, I opened up collaboration with a bare repository. When web apps couldn’t reach local files, I built a local adapter that asks the user first.

  4. 04

    Follow the evidence, not what I built

    The LightGBM I tuned myself won once, but I picked Chronos-2 for production because it held up when the evaluation period changed.

  5. 05

    Don’t let an unexplained number slide

    Before deleting outliers, find out why they’re there (office districts, floating population). When training diverges, reread the logs before changing settings.

  6. 06

    Building it isn’t the end

    Moving Excel into a database doesn’t make anyone’s job easier until they can work with that data through AI. So I assist Claude Code training for team leads, executives, and selected business staff (AI Frontier), run Databricks training for a business unit, and visit plants to find work worth automating.
